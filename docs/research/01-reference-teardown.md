# Reference teardown: how marketing sites win the first 3 seconds

Branch `research/assessment`. Researched 22 October 2026.

**Question:** what do a visitor's eyes land on in the first screen of each site, what do those first words make them believe, and which persuasion mechanism is at work?

## 1. Knowvia: the benchmark
**First screen (1440×900):**
- H1 "Health insurance, read before the hospital does."
- Sub-line (one sentence): what it does, person by person.
- One email field and one button.
- "Supported by" with two logos.
- On the right, an **example card** showing the product working: "Page 2, line 4: sum insured ₹5,00,000".

| What it does | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| The category noun comes first ("Health insurance"). The outcome comes second ("before the hospital does"). | The reader has classified the site within the first two words. That is processing fluency: easy-to-read claims are judged as more true.† |
| The right column shows the product doing its job, not a picture of people. | Show, don't tell. The visitor watches the promise being kept before reading further. |
| Stats are drawn from their own data: a proportional split bar, a 3 h vs 48 h bar, a 100-dot matrix for 43%. | The infographic *is* the number. No icons are needed, and every figure carries its source line. |
| "Same five moments, two ways": a 2-column comparison of the hospital desk at 2 a.m. versus any calm day with Knowvia. | Contrast, and loss aversion: the reader feels the cost of not having it. |
| Four stories, each one big number plus one line plus an attribution ("A., 26, Gurugram. Recorded conversation"). | Specific beats general. A named, dated source makes the story checkable. |
| "What we will not do": five lines. | Stating limits (the pratfall effect) raises trust in everything else. |
| Copy discipline: no paragraph over about 25 words, and every section heading is a full sentence. | The headings alone tell the whole story to someone who only scans. |

## 2. Rishav Dewan: a peer's personal portfolio
**First screen:**
- Availability chip.
- Kicker: "Product judgment. Technical direction. Real execution."
- H1: **"AI Product Builder"** (the role, in the biggest type).
- One-line scope: "ambiguous problems → usable AI systems" across named domains.
- Two hero numbers: 5M+ and $40k.
- Geography line, then a photo.

| Works | Doesn't |
| --- | --- |
| The role is the H1, so a recruiter files him in under a second. | "5M+ potential product reach" is a vanity metric; "potential" weakens it. |
| "Built. Changed. Measured." Each project card leads with a number and states the result *before* the description. | The hero and sections are hidden until scroll animations run. In our test the screen was blank mid-scroll. |
| Named organisations (Scale AI, Newton School, ALLEN) carry the authority. | Emoji icons and long rows of tag pills look like a template. |

## 3. OWLED Media (Ayush Wadhwa)
**First screen:**
- H1 "We operate on overdrive." (attitude, no information).
- Under it, the sub-line does the work, with **bolded keywords**: "**AI-powered strategy**, human storytelling, and **unmatched scale**".
- A red "Schedule a call" button sits in both corners.

| Mechanism | Note for us |
| --- | --- |
| An attitude headline plus an information sub-line | This only works when the sub-line is visible in the same glance. Pritam's current site has the attitude headline but the sub-line is pushed below the fold. |
| Scale proof: "over 700 videos a month", "150+ in-house" | Big round numbers signal capacity. We cannot invent these. Our honest equivalent is named artists and companies. |
| "We build Videos / Campaigns / IPs" | Three nouns are the whole service menu. |
| CTA copy "Bring us the problem, we'll bring the clarity." | The CTA is phrased as a trade, so the visitor knows what they give and what they get. |
| Condensed caps, near-black, one red | Already close to Pritam's current look, so the visual language suits the audience. |

## 4. Schbang (agency)
- H1 is pure positioning: "Your Creative & Technology Transformation Partner."
- The sub-line is all proof: "1000+ Specialists … 350+ brands … 11 years".
- Case studies are **titled by their result**: "193M+ Views".

The lesson: the case-study headline is the result, and the client name is the subtitle.

## 5. Harry Dry, Marketing Examples (independent creator)
- Social proof sits in the hero: "#1 marketing newsletter", "130k people".
- Titles follow a pattern of two beats and a number:
  - "Same interview. 700x reach."
  - "This rebrand cost Tropicana $55M"
  - "One of these videos has 18M views"
- His copywriting test (from his guide)† is three questions: *Can you visualise it? Can you falsify it? Can nobody else say it?*

That test fits Pritam well:
- "The pictures sell the next one" fails the visualise and falsify tests.
- "Fakira, Cactus, Fossils, Jeet Ganguly, Monali Thakur, Anupam Roy, shot from the pit" passes all three.

## 6. Anurag Bansal (Decoding Brands)
- His whole site is one line: **"I study why brands work."** It states the identity as a verb plus its object, with no adjectives.
- His headlines put a **familiar name next to a contradiction**, then the sub-line adds one hard number:
  - "How A Cigarette Company Became One Of India's Most Trusted FMCG Brands", with the sub-line "…sells about 80% of India's cigarettes"
  - "RCB - The Million-Dollar Brand Without a Trophy?"
  - "Why most Indian celebrity brands fail (and why a few don't)", with the sub-line "1,000+ crore"
- The authority comes from the names he is associated with ("Ex - Zerodha | Masters' Union"), not from describing himself.

The pattern for Pritam: put names people know (the artists) beside the unexpected angle, a photographer who thinks like the marketing team.

## 7. Candid Shutters (concert-photography agency)
- The case study opens with a **spec sheet**:
  - Client: Unboxed Events
  - Brand: Coca-Cola India
  - City: Chandigarh
  - Crew: 2 photographers
  - Turnaround: 7 days
- The photos are framed as "rich visual content for social media".

The lesson: people who buy event photography read it as a spec (who, for whom, how fast, how many). Pritam's case studies should open with that table.

## The psychology underneath (research)
| Principle | Evidence | Rule for this site |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The first ~10 seconds decide whether the visitor stays | NN/g, "How Long Do Users Stay on Web Pages?" (Liu et al., Microsoft Research: 205,873 pages, Weibull dwell-time curve). Most leaving happens early, then the curve flattens after about 30 seconds. | Who, what and proof must all be in the first viewport. Nothing important waits for a scroll or an animation. |
| Visual impressions form in about 50 ms | Lindgaard et al., 2006, *Behaviour & IT*† | The first visual must be the actual work (a concert frame), not an empty block or a slow fade. |
| People scan in an F-pattern and read the first words of each line | NN/g eye-tracking, 2006† | Front-load the key nouns: "Concert photographer…", "Fakira, Cactus…". |
| Processing fluency | Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, 2004† | Plain words. Put the category noun first. No riddles in the H1. |
| Concrete language is judged more truthful | Hansen & Wänke, 2010† | Use names, counts and places, not adjectives. |
| Social proof and authority | Cialdini, *Influence*† | Recognisable artists and the two production companies appear in the first screen. |
| Picture superiority effect | Paivio's dual-coding research† | Use real frames as the infographic material, not flat icons. |
| Hick's law | Hick, 1952† | One primary action and one secondary action in the hero. |
| Von Restorff (isolation) effect | Restorff, 1933† | One accent colour, used only for the single most important element in each view. |

† Cited from prior knowledge. These sources were not re-opened during this research.

## The seven rules this teardown produces
1. **Make the H1 a classification, not a riddle.** Category noun first, outcome second (Knowvia, Rishav, Schbang).
2. **Show the work doing its job beside the H1.** For Pritam, that means a real concert frame annotated with who each part of it is for. This is the counterpart of Knowvia's example card.
3. **Put the names in the first screen:** six artists and two production companies (Anurag Bansal, Schbang, Rishav).
4. **Draw the infographics from the data itself:** bars whose length is the frame count, made of the actual frames (Knowvia). No icons and no stock symbols.
5. **Give every number a source,** computed from the content files, and never invent one.
6. **Title each case study by what it delivered, and open it with a spec table** (Schbang, Candid Shutters).
7. **Keep it scan-proof.** Sentence headings, ≤ 25-word paragraphs, two buttons, nothing behind an animation.

## Anti-patterns to avoid (seen or common)
- Bento grids, glass panels, gradient blobs, emoji or line icons, tag-pill walls.
- Content hidden until a scroll animation fires.
- Vanity or "potential" metrics.
- Clever headlines with the explanation below the fold.
